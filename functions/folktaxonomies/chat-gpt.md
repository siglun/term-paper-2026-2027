I had a chat with a Danish botanist (turned software engineer). He claimed that common weeds in gardening and farming had more seldom names that were common between Swedish and Danish, than more rare plants (that were more likely to be discussed when Scandinavian biologists met on conferences and the like). It sounds plausible, but is there data that supports it?

--------

I think your Danish botanist's hypothesis is genuinely interesting. It connects three things that are often studied separately: the frequency with which people encounter a plant, the history of its vernacular name, and the social networks through which knowledge travels.

My preliminary conclusion is that there is evidence for parts of the hypothesis, but I haven't found a study that directly tests the specific Swedish–Danish comparison he proposed. The idea is plausible, but it is worth separating what the existing research establishes from what remains conjectural.

## 1. The underlying idea

Imagine comparing two groups of plants:

- Everyday weeds: plants encountered repeatedly in gardens, fields and allotments. Their names are passed between neighbours, farmers, gardeners and generations of speakers.
- Uncommon plants: plants encountered less frequently, perhaps discussed mainly by botanists, conservationists or specialists. Their names may be learned from books, floras and scientific meetings.

The intriguing possibility is that the first group preserves more shared traditional vocabulary across Swedish and Danish, while the second group has names that are more often independently established, borrowed from scientific terminology, or standardised separately.

There is a plausible historical mechanism here. Swedish and Danish speakers have a long history of linguistic contact, and everyday agricultural knowledge could have circulated across the linguistic boundary long before modern botanical conferences existed.

But there is an important distinction: a plant can have a shared name because the name is old and inherited, because it has spread between languages, or because the two languages independently developed similar descriptive names. Those are different historical processes.

## 2. What the research does tell us

Three strands of research are especially relevant.

A. Folk plant names and the importance of plants

A discussion of Swedish folk plant nomenclature, drawing on earlier work by Sigurd Fries and others, reports a striking proposition attributed to the linguist Dahlstedt: important plants tend to have fewer names, whereas less important plants tend to have more. The proposed explanation is that people use a more stable, widely recognised name for plants that matter to them. However, the discussion also gives counterexamples, so this is not a universal rule.&#x20;

B. Research explicitly comparing Nordic plant names

The Swedish Institute for Language and Folklore (Institutet för språk och folkminnen) published Aspekter på växtnamn (1997), a collection of papers on plant names, dialects and folk knowledge. Its topics include Nordic names for cotton grasses, the relationship between plant names and cultural history, and the naming of plants in different languages.&#x20;

This is a promising place to look for historical evidence. It demonstrates that the relevant questions have a research tradition, although I haven't established that any paper in this collection tests your particular hypothesis.

C. Cross-language naming and historical plant knowledge

A study published in Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B in 2021 combines linguistic, archaeological, historical and ethnobotanical evidence to investigate plant use in Viking-Age Scandinavia. It compares 12 plant species and finds that plants for which ancestral uses could be inferred had vernacular names with shared North Germanic roots. Plants without such inferred uses tended to have less consistent naming traditions, or more restricted geographical distributions.&#x20;

## 3. Why the botanist's explanation is plausible — and where it could go wrong

The conference explanation is particularly interesting because it suggests two different social mechanisms for transmitting names.

Everyday transmission

Gardeners and farmers encounter the same plants repeatedly. They learn names through family, neighbours and local practice. Shared names may persist even when nobody has consciously standardised them.

Expert transmission

Botanists exchange knowledge through floras, taxonomic literature, professional societies and conferences. This can spread terminology across borders, but it can also promote standardised names that differ between national traditions.

The crucial qualification is that scientific contact can produce either convergence or divergence. If Swedish and Danish botanists use the same international scientific terminology, expert communication may make their names more similar, not less.

There are also at least three possible confounders:

- Plant distribution: a rare plant in one country might be common in another, and some plants occur only in particular habitats.
- Taxonomic attention: rare plants may have several vernacular names precisely because specialists have studied them extensively.
- The meaning of “shared”: Swedish maskros and Danish mælkebøtte, for example, are the standard names for the dandelion in the respective languages, but they are not the same word. Should a comparison count only historically related names, or also names with similar meanings?

These distinctions matter if we want to test the claim rather than merely find examples that seem to confirm it.

## 4. How could we test it properly?

I would approach this as a small comparative study in historical linguistics and ecology.

1. Assemble a list of Swedish and Danish vernacular names from historical plant-name dictionaries and dialect archives, linking each name to a botanical species wherever possible.
2. Measure how common each species is, ideally using field records or agricultural surveys rather than simply classifying plants as common or rare.
3. For each species, calculate the proportion of its recorded Swedish and Danish vernacular names that are shared or demonstrably cognate—that is, descended from the same historical word.
4. Test whether that proportion is higher for common weeds, controlling for geographical distribution, plant habitat, historical use and the number of names recorded for each species.

It would also be worth distinguishing names that are genuinely inherited from older Scandinavian vocabulary from names that have spread through modern botanical standardisation. Otherwise, the analysis could mistake the effect of professional communication for the effect of everyday contact.

## My verdict

The hypothesis is plausible, and the literature gives us reasons to take seriously the relationship between plant importance, shared knowledge and the stability of vernacular names. But I would not yet claim that the particular Swedish–Danish pattern has been demonstrated.

The most useful next step would be to examine the historical Nordic plant-name literature and its underlying dictionaries, especially the material cited in Aspekter på växtnamn and the work on shared North Germanic plant names. That could establish whether the relevant data already exist in a form that would make your botanist's observation testable.

And I rather like the idea itself: it asks whether the social ecology of language leaves a different signature from the social ecology of scientific expertise.
